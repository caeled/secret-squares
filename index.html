What makes a code scan well?

Dark modules, a white background, and a clear four-module border. Keep it flat, large enough, and sharply printed. Adding a logo over modules can make scanning fail.

inside the squares.

Find the code, know its orientation, sample the grid, remove a mask, recover the message. Explore your current code.

How the message becomes a grid
  1. Choose numeric, alphanumeric, or byte segments; declare UTF-8 when needed.
  2. Add mode, length, data bits, and padding.
  3. Calculate Reed–Solomon error-correction codewords.
  4. Interleave blocks and place bits in a zigzag through unreserved modules.
  5. Apply a mask to avoid patterns that are difficult to read.
  6. Add protected format information specifying the mask and correction level.

A mask is not encryption. The payload area combines message bits, recovery bits, and remainder bits.

Recovery has limits

Reed–Solomon correction adds mathematical redundancy. L, M, Q, and H are commonly described as recovering roughly 7%, 15%, 25%, and 30% of codewords. These are not guaranteed percentages of image area you can cover.

Distribution across blocks, structural marks, print quality, and the reader all matter. Covering a finder may defeat detection even with much of the data intact.

The patch changes actual modules. jsQR tests the result locally. This is not a guaranteed scanning threshold for every phone.

Why QR capacity changes
Numeric mode packs three digits into ten bits. QR alphanumeric mode packs pairs from its 45-character alphabet into eleven bits. Byte mode carries bytes. Mode headers, length fields, correction level, and QR version also affect capacity.
A QR code does not automatically gzip your words. The studio chooses a QR segment mode; this bench separately measures actual gzip. It does not put compressed data into an ordinary text code.

THE SECRET-MESSAGE BENCH / PUBLIC PATTERN, PRIVATE MEANING
Everyone can scan.
Who can read?
A QR code is a container, not a lock. Encrypt first, then encode the encrypted envelope.
Two ways to share
Shared key: keep the same random secret on both sides. Share the encrypted code publicly and give the key privately through a separate trusted channel.
Public key: publish the receiving key. Anyone can encrypt to its owner; the private key stays with the owner. Confirm who owns the public key through a trusted check.
Educational AES-256-GCM and RSA-OAEP/SHA-256 experiments, not an audited messaging service. Use invented messages. Encryption hides content, not size, timing, recipients, or the existence of a message. This workshop does not authenticate the sender.

Why repeated messages make different envelopes
Each encryption uses a fresh random 12-byte nonce. AES-GCM creates an authentication tag: changing encrypted bytes, nonce, or key causes verification to fail. Public-key messages use a fresh AES key wrapped with the recipient’s RSA public key. These are established algorithms, not a homemade letter-shifting cipher.
Public-key post office
Make a receiving pair. Share the public key; keep the private one. To receive later, intentionally save the private key on your device.
Keys live in memory only. Save the private key before refreshing if you need it later.
Compare the full SHA-256 public-key fingerprint through a trusted channel. A fingerprint helps confirm a key, but does not establish identity alone. Exported private keys are unencrypted files: protect them.

Order, loss, and changed data
Each custom packet carries its number, total count, and the complete message’s SHA-256 checksum. The receiver handles different order, ignores identical duplicates, and reports missing or inconsistent pieces. A checksum is not authentication: an attacker can replace an entire message and its checksum.
These are ordinary QR symbols containing our custom SSP1 text—not QR Structured Append. A regular scanner reads each code; this workshop understands reassembly. Packet text is not encrypted.
UTF-8 characters may split between packets. Join the bytes before decoding and emoji can survive the journey.
