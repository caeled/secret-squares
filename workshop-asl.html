A HUMAN CONNECTION / BEYOND MACHINE CODES

Messages can be seen.
So can language.

Learn a small part of American Sign Language: its fingerspelled alphabet. Then discover a language and community that go far beyond spelling.

A language, not a secret code

ASL has its own grammar. Hands, facial expression, and movement all carry meaning. Fingerspelling helps with names and some English words; spelling letters is not the same as signing a sentence.

ASL developed within communities over time, with influences from local and French sign languages. It is not universal: other regions use different sign languages. For many Deaf people it is everyday language, culture, and connection.

3. Try a tiny exchange

  1. Choose an invented three-letter name, such as SAM.
  2. Find the handshapes in the reference and watch their movement.
  3. Try fingerspelling to a partner, without rushing. Ask what they understood.
  4. Then study a real ASL lesson to discover how signing a thought differs from spelling an English sentence.

A visual language is still a language. Learning a few letters does not qualify someone to interpret. Follow Deaf educators and let curiosity grow into respectful practice.

One more way to connect

You’ve learned a new way to share meaning. For many people, this is everyday language—and an invitation to connect.
